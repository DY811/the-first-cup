The First Cup

You drink it every day.
You have no idea what it is.
Neither did I.

Scroll ↓

You wake up and you want coffee.

Before you get it you have to find a door, order something, watch it being made, and finally enjoy it. Four steps.

I could not explain any of them properly. So I looked them all up. Then I found out that millions of other people look them up too.

01

Find it

Who found it first — and how do we find it?

A goat found it first

Goats ate the red berries and would not sleep. Their herder tried them himself and took them to the monks. In the story he is called Kaldi.

The story is set in the ninth century. Nobody wrote it down until 1671. In that first version, the herder has no name.2

His name Kaldi was added later. Ethiopia's biggest coffee chain is called Kaldi's. There is a Kaldi's Coffee in St. Louis too, and Kaldi Coffee Farm shops across Japan.9

03

Watch it

Why does the same bean taste different?

Pressure, time, and what the filter keeps

Each device is placed by how hard it pushes, and how long the water and coffee stay together.11

The filter determines whether coffee oils reach the cup. Paper traps them; metal and cloth do not.10

The moka pot is named after Mokha, a port in Yemen. So is the caffè mocha.6

How do you put a taste into words?

The vocabulary of a sip

A grader never writes “good”. They write blackcurrant, cane sugar, wet cardboard, tobacco, cooked butter, sour milk.5 The list is fixed and published. Anyone can borrow it. Try it on the cup next to you. Not “nice”. What does it actually taste like?

Not everyone drinks it every day

Ten of these sixty-seven countries drink a cup a day. Nineteen do not reach one cup a week.

A cup is 12 g of green coffee, about 10 g roasted.34 The dashed line sits at seven cups, which is one a day. Norway is highest at 14 a week, and Angola lowest at one cup every 40 weeks.

Data, method and notes

Sources

  1. Faustus Nairon, De saluberrima potione cahue, seu cafe nuncupata discursus (Rome, 1671). Nairon was a Maronite priest who taught Syriac at the Sapienza in Rome; this is the earliest known printed account of the goat legend. Historians treat the tale as apocryphal: it embellishes the better-supported tradition that Sufi communities encountered coffee in Ethiopia, across the Red Sea from Yemen. Three details drifted after 1671 and are worth knowing: Nairon sets the story in Yemen rather than Ethiopia; the ninth-century date belongs to the legend rather than to any record; and in Nairon's account the herder does not taste the berries at all — he is annoyed that his goats will not settle, and walks to the monastery to complain, where the monks are the ones who make the drink. We call it a legend throughout this page, because that is what it is. Public domain. Internet Archive

  6. Recipe definitions. Fifteen drinks, each one placed by what the source says it holds. The espresso follows the World Barista Championship rules: a beverage of around 30 ml, poured from one side of a double portafilter in one continuous extraction. The championship defines no other drink, so for the others we use the definition from the country it comes from, or the encyclopaedia entry for it. Ristretto 20 ml from a double basket. Doppio 60 ml. Lungo 50 to 70 ml. Americano espresso and hot water at 1:3 to 1:4, 120 to 180 ml in total. Long black 100 to 120 ml of water, less than an americano, which is what makes it taste stronger. Espresso macchiato 5 to 10 g of milk. Cortado espresso with a roughly equal amount of warm milk, and no foam. Cortadito equal parts again, on sweetened Cuban espresso, with the espumita on top. Café au lait one part brewed coffee to one part frothed milk. Flat white and latte the same amount of coffee, around 30 ml, but a flat white is served in a 175 ml cup and a latte in a 225 ml cup. Latte macchiato half an espresso shot or less. Cappuccino the Istituto Nazionale Espresso Italiano certifies 25 ml of espresso with 100 ml of milk, steam-foamed until it reaches about 125 ml, served in a 150 to 160 ml cup: the 25 ml the milk gains is air, so a sixth of the cup is foam. Caffè mocha a latte with chocolate added. Foam is drawn only where a source gives an amount. That is three drinks: cappuccino, the 5 mm of microfoam on a flat white, and the espumita on a cortadito. The sources for the others either say there is no foam or say nothing. Two amounts are not published and we drew them small: the chocolate in a mocha, and the espumita. Recipes vary. Commercial chains in the United States often serve a cappuccino at 360 ml or more. A flat white comes in a 175 ml cup in New Zealand and a 200 ml cup in Australia. Cortado ratios change from region to region. Every barista pours a little differently.

  8. Brewing pressure and time. The times come from the National Coffee Association brewing guides, which state a brewing time for each method: espresso 20 to 30 seconds, pour-over 2 to 4 minutes, French press about 4 minutes, cold brew about 12 hours. NCA brewing guides The World Barista Championship 2025 rules give the same 20 to 30 seconds for espresso, at 8.5 to 9.5 bar. The moka pot reaches 1 to 2 bar. The AeroPress reaches 0.25 to 0.5 bar over 30 to 60 seconds: its inventor Alan Adler calculated that figure and the maker publishes it. The National Coffee Association has no page for the AeroPress, so we use the maker. Two devices have no published time at all. Bialetti's instructions for the moka pot and the standard account of the cezve both end on a cue, not a clock, so their positions on the time scale are approximate.
  9. Coffee oils by brewing method. The oils carry two diterpenes, cafestol and kahweol. Moreira and colleagues measured them across brewing methods and report boiled coffee highest, at 232 mg of cafestol esters per litre, then the French press; the moka pot at 36 and espresso at 10 to 54; and paper-filtered and instant coffee lowest: Variability of some diterpene esters in coffee beverages as influenced by brewing procedures, Journal of Food Science and Technology. An earlier review states the mechanism: the diterpenes dissolve in hot water but a paper filter retains them, which is why filtered coffee does not raise cholesterol while boiled, cafetière and Turkish coffees do: The cholesterol-raising factor from coffee beans, Annual Review of Nutrition, 1997. Accounts differ on how completely paper holds the oils back. A 2017 review reports paper-filtered coffee raising cholesterol in some studies. Cold brew carries no mark on the chart because it is strained with paper, a metal sieve or felt.

Its representative values are drawn from published ranges for green coffee beans. A 2023 open-access review reports Arabica dry-matter ranges including 10–14% triglycerides, 12% proteins, 6.2–7.9% chlorogenic acids, 5.3–9.3% sugars and 0.6–1.5% caffeine, alongside several major polysaccharide groups: Coffee Roasting, Blending, and Grinding: Nutritional, Sensorial and Sustainable Aspects, Agriculture, 2023. An open-access review in Nutrients independently summarizes green beans as roughly 50–60% carbohydrates, 12–18% lipids, 11–13% proteins, 6–8% chlorogenic acids and 0.5–1.5% caffeine: Coffee and Endothelial Function: A Coffee Paradox?, 2019. Water and minor components vary with species, origin and processing, so the displayed shares are normalized and deliberately rounded.

What we assume

  1. USDA reports coffee as green beans. We count one cup as 12 g of green coffee, about 10 g roasted.13
  2. Recipes differ from shop to shop. We drew what the published definition says, not what your local café pours.
  3. Lookups count page views. They are not people, and they are not popularity.
  4. Page views are counted by language edition, not by country. Someone reading the English page may live anywhere.
  5. The European Union reports coffee as one bloc, so its member states are missing from the last figure.
  6. The last figure groups countries into rows. A national average does not hold to tenths of a cup a week.
  7. Accessibility. Every image carries alt text, and the two decorative ones carry none on purpose. All five charts carry a written title and description for screen readers. Red marks emphasis only, never a data category. Animation stops when your system asks for less motion.
